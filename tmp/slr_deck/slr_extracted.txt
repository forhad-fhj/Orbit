Strengthening Maternal and Child Health Awareness
Among Sylhet’s Tea-Garden Families Through a
CHW-Assisted mHealth Application: A Systematic
Review of the Evidence
Md. Forhad Hasan Jewel and Muhammad Milon
Abstract
Families living and working on Sylhet’s tea estates continue to experience some of the poor-
est maternal and child health outcomes in Bangladesh, a situation driven jointly by physical
isolation, widespread illiteracy, weak health infrastructure, and long-standing cultural norms
surrounding childbirth. This review synthesises evidence drawn from 45 peer-reviewed articles
published between 2017 and 2024, retrieved from Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, the ACM Digital
Library, PubMed, and ScienceDirect. Our aim was to establish what is currently known – and,
just as importantly, what remains unknown – about pairing community health worker (CHW)
support with mobile health technology to raise maternal and child health awareness in settings
comparable to Sylhet’s plantations. Several patterns surfaced repeatedly across the literature:
SMS- and voice-based appointment reminders tend to lift antenatal care (ANC) attendance
and immunization coverage; Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) approaches built around im-
agery, spoken narration, and local dialects consistently serve low-literacy audiences better than
text-heavy alternatives; and CHWs repeatedly emerge as the connective tissue that makes any
mHealth tool actually usable by the people it targets. At the same time, the evidence base
specific to plantation communities in Bangladesh is remarkably thin – we located essentially
no published study that has piloted an mHealth intervention inside a Sylhet tea garden itself.
Building on what the literature does offer, we sketch a feature-level architecture – icon-based
checklists, Bangla- and Chattisheuli-language audio, offline functionality, and a dashboard for
CHWs – while underscoring that participatory, ground-level research must precede any real
deployment.
1 Introduction
In 2017 alone, an estimated 295,000 women died during pregnancy or shortly afterward, and nearly
94 percent of those deaths occurred in countries where health systems are already under severe strain
[1]. Bangladesh, to its credit, has narrowed its maternal mortality ratio considerably between 2000
and 2020. Yet national averages tend to flatten out important regional variation. Once the lens
narrows to Sylhet division – and further still, to its tea estates – the outlook becomes noticeably
harsher.
Roughly 360,000 workers and family members live on these plantations, and women and girls
account for close to two-thirds of the workforce [2]. Daily wages sit around BDT 187, an amount that
barely stretches to cover a carbohydrate-heavy diet, let alone the nutritional needs of a pregnant
woman. Many residents speak Sylheti or Chattisheuli rather than standard Bangla, functional
literacy is limited, and where clinics exist at all, they are frequently understaffed and physically

hard to reach [43, 44].
Set against this, Bangladesh now has more than 180 million active mobile subscriptions. Even in
isolated rural pockets, phone ownership – feature phones especially, and increasingly smartphones
too – is no longer unusual. That raises a fairly direct question: could mobile technology, combined
with trusted local health workers, be shaped into a practical way of closing the maternal-health
information gap in places like tea gardens?
This is far from a novel idea. mHealth pilots have proliferated across South Asia and Sub-Saharan
Africa over the last ten years [3, 16]. What has received far less attention is how HCI principles
should inform the design of such tools for users who may not be able to read a menu label, who
share a single household phone among several family members, and who lose signal every time it
rains [6, 5].
This paper sets out to map what is currently known. We examine what CHW-supported mHealth
programmes have delivered in broadly comparable settings, what HCI research recommends for
designing around low literacy, and what the specific health circumstances of Sylhet’s tea-garden
families require of any proposed solution. We are also candid about where the evidence thins out.
Our objective is to hand researchers and practitioners a grounded starting point – not a ready-made
blueprint.
2 Background
2.1 What mHealth Looks Like in Maternal and Child Health
“mHealth,” broadly defined, refers to any use of mobile or wireless technology in service of health
goals [13]. Within maternal health specifically, the applications tend to cluster into a few recogniz-
able types. The most basic send timed SMS nudges – a reminder that an ANC visit is coming up, or
that a vaccination is due. A step up from that, CHWs are equipped with structured digital check-
lists on a phone or tablet to guide home visits. At the far end of the spectrum sit more elaborate
systems combining decision-support logic, referral pathways, and dashboards for supervisors.
Across multiple reviews, a consistent pattern emerges: these interventions, even the simpler ones,
tend to produce results [9, 35]. ANC attendance climbs. Vaccination completion improves. More
births happen with a skilled attendant present. The catch is that most of this evidence comes from
controlled pilots backed by dedicated funding and technical support. What happens once the NGO
withdraws and the hardware starts failing is a question the literature mostly leaves unanswered.
Figure 1 illustrates the network of stakeholders involved in any tea-garden mHealth effort. No
single party can carry it alone.
2.2 Health Conditions Inside Sylhet’s Tea Gardens
On-the-ground reporting paints a stark picture. A 2025 investigation by The Daily Star found that
95 percent of surveyed tea workers reported issues such as anaemia, stunted growth among children,
night blindness, or recurring skin infections [43]. Among households with a pregnant woman, not
a single respondent reported adjusting their diet to account for the pregnancy. Average monthly
household income stood at BDT 6,995 – for a family of six.
Antenatal visits happen rarely, and postnatal check-ups even less so. Vaccination schedules fre-
quently slip, either because mothers are unaware of the dates or because reaching a clinic is simply

2.3 Why HCI Carries Extra Weight Here
Designing a health app for a smartphone-literate university graduate in Dhaka is a very different
exercise from designing one for a 22-year-old tea plucker who recognizes perhaps forty written
words, shares a single low-end Android device with her husband, and lives somewhere 3G coverage
is unreliable at best.
HCI research on low-literacy users points toward several requirements that are not optional extras
[6, 5]. Text needs to be replaced, or at minimum supplemented, by pictures – and not decorative
ones, but functional pictograms that communicate meaning independently. Voice narration should
accompany what appears on screen, ideally delivered in the user’s own dialect rather than formal
Bangla. Menu structures need to stay flat, since every additional tap introduces a new opportunity
for confusion. And the entire system has to function without a live connection, because connectivity
simply cannot be assumed.
None of this is guesswork. A four-country comparison led by Medhi found that first-time, low-
literacy participants essentially could not operate text-driven menus, while graphical and voice-
guided versions of the same tasks pushed completion rates up to a workable level [6]. Translate
that into a tea-garden setting – where many users may never have handled a smartphone before –
and the design constraints become fairly explicit.

2.4 What the Evidence Says About Outcomes
Even though no study has tested this precise CHW-plus-mHealth combination inside a Sylhet tea
garden, it is possible to assemble a reasonably clear picture of what such interventions tend to
accomplish elsewhere.
ANC attendance.Mobile reminders were linked to a 20-to-40-percent rise in attendance across
several rural South Asian and African trials, and among low-literacy participants voice messages
consistently outdid plain text [25, 35]. That is a sizeable shift on paper – whether it holds once a
pilot’s funding and support disappear is a separate, and largely open, question.
Vaccination.The evidence is both stronger and better quantified. A meta-analysis combining 14
randomized trials and 4 quasi-experimental studies across Africa found that mHealth interventions
roughly doubled the odds of a child being vaccinated on schedule (OR 2.15; 95% CI: 1.70–2.72)
[36]. Uddin and colleagues reported a comparable effect specifically within rural Bangladesh, where
SMS reminders raised vaccination uptake by a factor of 3.6 in hard-to-reach villages [10].
CHW effectiveness.A Bangladeshi cluster-randomized trial found that tablet-based job aids
allowed community health workers to sustain visit coverage above 90 percent throughout long-
duration maternal follow-up – a level that, absent the app, would plausibly have been much harder
to maintain given the logistics of tracking dozens of mother-child pairs across dispersed households
[4].
Health knowledge.Interactive pictorial tools and voice-driven education modules were asso-
ciated with measurable improvements in mothers’ ability to recognize pregnancy and postnatal
danger signs, along with a better grasp of basic nutrition and newborn care [26, 40]. Whether that
knowledge holds up beyond a few months, however, remains under-researched.

Equity.CHW-delivered mHealth stands out as one of relatively few intervention models that gen-
uinely appears to reach “last-mile” populations – including urban slum residents, remote villagers,
and potentially plantation workers – rather than disproportionately benefiting those who already
have decent access to care [22, 33].
3 Research
3.1 How This Review Was Conducted
We used a modified PRISMA process spread across four stages. Structured search queries were first
run across five databases between November 2024 and January 2025. Retrieved records were then
screened by title. Records that survived that pass were screened again using their abstracts and
conclusions. Finally, the remaining papers were read in full, and those satisfying every inclusion
criterion were kept for thematic analysis. Two reviewers handled screening; where they disagreed,
the matter was resolved through discussion rather than referred to a third-party adjudicator – a
limitation we acknowledge in our methodology.
3.2 The Questions Guiding This Review
Three questions shaped how we read the literature:
1. What usability barriers and socio-technical friction points do CHWs and tea-garden mothers
face – or would plausibly face – when using an mHealth tool for maternal and child health
guidance?
2. How might a CHW-mediated, low-literacy, audio-enabled mobile interface improve health
awareness, support retention of what mothers learn, and encourage timely care-seeking?
3. Among the commonly discussed HCI features – picture-based checklists, audio prompts, offline
functionality, CHW-facing dashboards, and regional-language labelling – which prove most
consequential for usability, building trust, and sustaining long-term adoption?

3.3.2 Inclusion and Exclusion
A paper made the cut only if it had gone through peer review, was published in English sometime
between 2017 and 2024, and engaged in a substantive way with at least one of four themes: mHealth
for maternal or child health, CHW-led delivery of care in resource-constrained settings, HCI or
usability research aimed at low-literacy users, or the health circumstances of tea-estate communities.
We generally left out non-peer-reviewed grey literature – UN programme reports were the one
exception, kept because they supplied Sylhet-specific context we could not find elsewhere – along
with duplicate entries and work situated entirely in high-income countries.

4 Analysis
Five thematic clusters emerged from our reading of the 45 retained papers. Each is presented
below, noting both where the supporting evidence is solid and where it grows thin.
Figure 4: How the 45 papers distributed across intervention themes
1. CHWs are not a nice-to-have – they are the linchpin.A recurring finding across
Bangladesh, India, Nigeria, and multiple East African programmes is that routing content through
a trained local worker beat pushing it straight to end users every time [22, 33]. Her value went well
past relaying messages: she rendered them in the right language, framed them in locally sensible
terms, and in doing so gave the content a credibility no phone screen could match on its own. In the
7

--- PAGE 8 ---
Bangladesh-based icddr,b trial, CHWs using a structured tablet application sustained visit coverage
above 90 percent over a multi-year follow-up period [4]. Similar models – the Aponjon service and
BRAC’s Manoshi programme among them – have already scaled to serve tens of thousands of
mothers in urban slums and the areas surrounding them, yet a formal test of either inside a tea
garden has still not happened [3].
One gap worth flagging: very few of the reviewed papers examined what happens to CHW motiva-
tion over time. Adding tasks to an app without adding compensation or recognition is a predictable
route to burnout. Morrison and colleagues identified supportive supervision and peer learning as
factors that sustained Bangladeshi CHWs through a multi-component child development interven-
tion, though that particular study did not involve an mHealth tool [18].
2. Making an interface usable when the reader cannot read.The HCI literature is
unambiguous on this point: conventional text-based menus simply do not work for non-literate
users. Medhi’s four-country study found that spoken-dialogue and graphical interfaces produced
dramatically higher task completion than text-based ones [6]. The GorbhoKotha prototype – a
Bangla-language pregnancy app built through user-centered design methods – showed that pictorial
symptom selectors combined with narrated voice screens could be understood even by women with
little formal schooling [14]. Nepal’s MANTRA project pushed this idea further still, embedding
health messages inside a simple mobile game and achieving engagement even among women who
had never previously touched a smartphone [20].
Sylhet’s tea gardens add a wrinkle most low-literacy research never has to confront: which language
is actually spoken at home. Formal Bangla is not that language for a large share of plantation
families. Alongside various tribal languages, Chattisheuli – spoken widely but never standardized in
written form – dominates everyday conversation. Build the interface in textbook Bangla, and it may
end up scarcely more usable than one built in English, unless the audio content genuinely reflects
how people talk. Nothing in the literature we reviewed takes on Chattisheuli-specific interface
design.
3. Voice works, but infrastructure often gets in the way.When Dissieka and colleagues
gave rural participants in Cˆ ote d’Ivoire a choice between SMS and voice reminders for vaccination
appointments, most opted for voice [31] – a preference broadly consistent with what one would
expect in oral-culture communities. Ghana’s MOTECH programme tells a less tidy story, though –
its voice messages reached fewer people than SMS did, undermined by calls that failed to connect,
congested networks, and basic handset constraints [27, 21].
The lesson for tea-garden design is not to abandon voice, but to pre-load it. Audio files that play
back offline – rather than being streamed or delivered via live call – would sidestep the connectivity
bottleneck entirely. This is a decision with implications spanning HCI, engineering, and cost.
We found no existing mHealth study that has tested pre-loaded Bangla or Sylheti-language audio
content within a plantation setting.
4. Reminders change behaviour, but context determines how much.Vaccination re-
minders represent the most mature sub-field within this review. Gilano and colleagues’ 2024 meta-
analysis places the effect firmly in meaningful territory: mHealth interventions roughly doubled
the likelihood of a child receiving scheduled vaccines on time [36]. Oliver-Williams’ earlier multi-
country review pointed in the same direction, though with effect sizes that varied considerably by
setting [8]. Within Bangladesh specifically, Uddin’s trial documented a 3.6-fold increase in rural
vaccination uptake linked to SMS reminders [10].

Translating this to tea gardens is not automatic, however. Phone sharing is common, and a re-
minder that lands on a husband’s device offers no guarantee the wife ever sees it. Low smartphone
penetration limits the reach of app-based push notifications. And the social hierarchy within plan-
tation life – where supervisors often control daily schedules and mobility – can override individual
intent to visit a clinic no matter how many reminders arrive.
5. Tea-garden-specific data exists, but only barely.The single most telling finding of this
review may be an absence rather than a presence: we could not locate a peer-reviewed study that
has actually trialled an mHealth intervention inside a Bangladeshi tea garden. Existing tea-garden
research tends to document the scale of the problem – malnutrition figures, ANC utilization rates,
labour conditions – rather than test a response to it [19, 43]. UN programme reports add useful
implementation narratives from Sylhet, but rarely carry the rigorous outcome data a design team
would need [2, 44].
That absence has real consequences. Any tool built for tea-garden families would inevitably be
extrapolated from settings that resemble, but do not replicate, plantation life. A rural Bangladeshi
village and a tea estate are not interchangeable – the two differ in how power is distributed, in
employer-run (and frequently under-resourced) health posts, in ethnic and linguistic composition,
and in how housing is clustered. Absent evidence gathered specifically in the gardens, even well-
meaning design decisions risk missing the mark.

5 What a Tea-Garden mHealth Tool Might Look Like
Drawing on the evidence reviewed above, we sketch a feature-level architecture. We want to be
clear that this is a proposal grounded in the literature, not a validated design. Field testing – ideally
carried out through participatory co-design involving tea-garden residents and CHWs – would need

6 Limitations of This Review
We think it is important to be upfront about what this review can, and cannot, claim.
First, our search was restricted to English-language databases. Relevant work published in Bangla,
Hindi, or other regional languages may well have been missed. This is a common blind spot across
global health reviews, and we have no reliable way of estimating its scale.

Second, this is a fast-moving field. New applications, trials, and design frameworks surface on what
feels like a monthly cadence. Because our search cut off in January 2025, nothing published after
that point could be included here.
Third, and probably the limitation that matters most: primary research on mHealth conducted
specifically within tea-garden settings is scarce to the point of being nearly absent. Much of what we
conclude about plantation families is pieced together from adjacent, but not equivalent, contexts –
rural villages elsewhere in Bangladesh, urban slums, farming communities in East Africa. Drawing
on those parallels is defensible, but readers should treat it as an inference rather than direct
evidence.
Fourth, study designs varied substantially across the reviewed papers. Some were large-sample
randomized controlled trials; others were small usability studies involving as few as ten participants.
We chose not to attempt a formal meta-analysis, since pooling such heterogeneous evidence would
produce figures that look precise without actually being meaningful.
Fifth, Sylhet’s tea gardens are both linguistically and ethnically distinctive. Findings drawn from
settings where the majority language matches the national language may not carry over cleanly to
a context where Chattisheuli or tribal languages dominate daily communication.
7 Where Research Should Go Next
7.1 Get Into the Gardens
No amount of desk-based review can substitute for sitting down with tea-farm mothers and CHWs,
understanding their actual needs, and co-designing solutions alongside them. Participatory design
workshops – genuine hands-on prototyping sessions, not just focus groups – should be the starting
point. Ideally, these sessions would generate Chattisheuli-language audio material and culturally
grounded visuals that no outside design team could produce on its own.

7.2 Map the Technology Landscape
Choosing a delivery platform – a native Android app, a progressive web app, USSD, or some blend
of these – should wait until someone has actually surveyed device ownership, handset types, and
network reliability across a representative slice of Sylhet’s plantations. The common claim that
smartphones are spreading fast deserves to be checked against what is true in these particular
communities rather than taken on faith. It would also be worth studying, carefully, how shared
phones actually function day to day: who holds the device, when family members can get to it,
and whether a message that arrives stays private.
7.3 Run Properly Designed Trials
At some point, only a proper field trial can settle whether a CHW-supported mHealth tool actually
moves the needle on maternal and child health in tea gardens. A cluster-randomized or stepped-
wedge design tracking outcomes for a year or more would suit this question well. And the outcomes
worth measuring go beyond ANC visits and vaccination counts – retention of health knowledge,
how quickly families seek care once a danger sign appears, and, just as importantly, how the added
workload affects CHWs themselves and whether they stay satisfied in the role.
7.4 Plan for Life After the Pilot
Sustainability remains the elephant in the room for mHealth research generally. Donor-funded
pilots end. Phones break. CHWs move on to other jobs. Any serious research agenda needs
to build in a handoff plan – to the government health system, to tea estate management, or to
community organizations – and needs to cost the ongoing operational requirements honestly rather
than optimistically.
7.5 Look Beyond Bangladesh’s Borders
Plantation health challenges are not unique to Bangladesh. Tea estates in Assam, rubber planta-
tions in Liberia, and sugar estates across Southern Africa face overlapping difficulties. Cross-setting
comparative research could help identify which elements of a tea-garden mHealth model are gen-
uinely locality-specific and which might generalize across plantation contexts more broadly.
8 Conclusion
We opened this review with a fairly concrete question: does the research literature suggest that
a CHW-backed mHealth app could genuinely raise maternal and child health awareness among
Sylhet’s tea-garden families? Our reading of the evidence points to a cautious yes, though several
caveats come attached.
Across low- and middle-income countries generally, the case for mHealth in maternal and child
health holds up well. ANC attendance and vaccination coverage both rise when mobile reminders
are involved. Delivery through a CHW beats messaging that goes straight to the end user, time and
again. Interfaces built around pictures and voice reach people that text-only tools leave behind.
And offline function paired with local-language support is not a nice-to-have – it is what makes any
of this work at all where infrastructure is thin. None of these points is seriously in dispute; they
show up again and again, across very different populations and geographies.

What remains missing is confirmation from inside a real Bangladeshi tea garden. These communities
carry a distinct combination of traits – incomes that sit far below already-low regional averages, the
Chattisheuli dialect, health posts run by the employer rather than the state, and labour roles sharply
divided by gender – that make borrowing conclusions from rural village research a reasonable move,
but not a risk-free one.
What we have sketched – checklists built around pictures, offline audio in Bangla and Chattisheuli,
a working mode that needs no live signal, vaccination tracking, alerts for danger signs, and a
dashboard the CHW can use – represents our best attempt to synthesize what the evidence points
toward. We do not, however, mistake a design built from literature for one ready to hand out. What
it is ready for is scrutiny: sitting down with tea-farm women and health workers, prototyping and
revising based on what they say, and only then moving to a properly controlled trial.
The stakes here are not abstract. In these communities, a vaccination that gets missed, a danger
sign nobody recognizes, or a delivery with no trained attendant present can cost a life, not merely
inconvenience a family. Given that mHealth appears capable of shifting those odds, adapting it
thoughtfully for tea-garden life is not an academic exercise for its own sake. It matters, plainly and
urgently.
